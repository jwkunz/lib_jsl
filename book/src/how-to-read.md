# How to read an entry

Every entry follows the same outline, so you can skim for what you need.

1. **Summary.** A box with the problem the algorithm solves, who devised it, its cost and
   accuracy, and links to the API items that implement it.
2. **Overview.** What the algorithm does and when you would choose it, in plain language.
3. **History.** Who created it, when, and what problem they were trying to solve.
4. **The idea.** The mathematical or technical argument for why the method works, with proofs
   where they are short and illuminating. This is the heart of each entry.
5. **Algorithm.** The procedure step by step, with its cost.
6. **Implementation.** The key lines of the library's code, taken directly from the source, with
   commentary on the numerical details that matter in practice.
7. **In practice.** Figures and tables generated by the library, strengths, weaknesses and
   common pitfalls.
8. **Exercises.** Problems that check and extend your understanding; some ask you to modify the
   example code.
9. **Further reading.** The original papers and the best textbook treatments.

## How to study an entry

The outline is the same everywhere, but the sections serve different purposes, and it helps to
read them differently.

- **Start with the Overview and stop.** It says what problem the algorithm solves and when you
  would use it. If that is all you need, the "Choosing a method" table in each chapter
  introduction will take you to the right entry faster than reading several.
- **Read "The idea" with pencil and paper.** This section is where the understanding is. It is
  written so that each step follows from the one before: a derivation is shown line by line, a
  new term is set in **bold** where it is defined, and most arguments are followed by a small
  worked example with actual numbers. Reproduce the example yourself before going on. If a step
  seems to come from nowhere, look back for the bold term or follow the link to the entry it
  depends on.
- **Use the figures and tables as experiments.** They are generated by the library, and the text
  beside each one says what to look for. Ask what the figure would look like if the claim in the
  text were false.
- **Treat "Algorithm" and "Implementation" as a second pass.** They are easier once the idea is
  clear, and the implementation notes often describe a mistake that is easy to make when coding the
  method. Those are worth reading closely: they are the mistakes you are likely to make too.
- **Do some exercises.** The early ones in each list check the derivations; the later ones ask
  you to run or change the example program.

Entries depend on each other loosely. Where one relies on an idea explained elsewhere (the
condition number, Newton's method, the convolution theorem), it links to the place where that
idea is developed. The DSP chapters share a short
[background section](filters/index.md#background-frequency-responses-and-transfer-functions) on
frequency responses and transfer functions.

## Notation

- $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the exact integral and $Q_n[f]$ an $n$-point approximation to it.
- $\mathbb{P}_n$ is the space of polynomials of degree at most $n$.
- $h$ is a step or panel width, and $O(h^p)$ means "bounded by a constant times $h^p$ as $h \to 0$".
- $\varepsilon \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ (see [Preliminaries](preliminaries.md#floating-point-arithmetic-and-the-three-kinds-of-error)) is the machine epsilon of IEEE double precision: the relative
  rounding error of a single arithmetic operation, so a double carries about 16 significant
  digits.
- Vectors are bold lower-case ($\mathbf x$), matrices upper-case ($A$); $A^{\mathsf T}$ is the
  transpose and $A^{\mathsf H}$ the conjugate transpose. $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert$ is the Euclidean
  length unless a subscript says otherwise.
- $O(n^2)$ applied to a cost means "at most a constant times $n^2$ operations for large $n$". A
  **flop** is one floating-point arithmetic operation.
- The imaginary unit is $i$, except in the signal-processing and electromagnetics chapters,
  which follow engineering practice and write $j$.

## Reading the figures

Convergence plots show error against the number of function evaluations, on logarithmic axes. On
such a plot an algebraic rate $\text{error} \propto N^{-p}$ is a straight line of slope $-p$,
while an exponential rate $\text{error} \propto e^{-cN}$ curves downwards ever more steeply.
Errors stop decreasing near $10^{-16}$, where rounding error in double precision dominates.
